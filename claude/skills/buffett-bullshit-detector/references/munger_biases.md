# Munger's 25 Standard Causes of Human Misjudgment

From Charlie Munger's "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" speech and *Poor Charlie's Almanack*. Munger argued that most bullshit, bad decisions, and human folly traces back to a small set of psychological tendencies — usually several acting in combination ("lollapalooza effects"). Knowing the names lets you spot them in real time.

Use this file when an incentive or psychology issue is dominant and the analysis needs the precise bias name. Each entry: what it is, how it shows up in pitches/people/arguments, and the antidote.

## 1. Reward and Punishment Superresponse Tendency (Incentive Bias)

**The king.** Munger: "I think I've been in the top 5% of my age cohort almost all my adult life in understanding the power of incentives, and yet I've always underestimated that power. Never a year passes but I get some surprise that pushes a little further my appreciation of incentive superpower."

**How it shows up:**
- Sell-side analysts paid by trading commissions issuing buy ratings
- Mortgage brokers paid per loan originating bad loans
- A doctor paid per procedure recommending a procedure
- An employee whose bonus depends on a non-GAAP metric defending that metric ferociously
- A politician's "principled position" lining up suspiciously with their donors

**Antidote:** Always ask first — *what is this person paid (in money, status, identity, or ego) to conclude?* Treat anyone's certainty as roughly inversely proportional to how much their compensation depends on the conclusion.

## 2. Liking/Loving Tendency

We distort facts and ignore faults in people, products, and organizations we like. Halo effect.

**How it shows up:**
- Charismatic CEOs getting a pass on bad capital allocation
- Founder cults around figures like Adam Neumann, Elizabeth Holmes, SBF — long after the evidence was visible
- "I love this company" preceding a $50K investment with no due diligence
- A friend's product gets glowing reviews you wouldn't give a stranger's

**Antidote:** Force yourself to evaluate the *thing* without the person attached. Would you buy this stock if Steve Jobs were not the CEO? Would you take this advice if your favorite uncle weren't giving it?

---

## 3. Disliking/Hating Tendency

The mirror image. We distort facts against people and organizations we dislike, refuse to learn from them, ignore their virtues.

**How it shows up:**
- Refusing to short a "good" company you admire even when the price is absurd
- Refusing to buy a "bad" company at a great price because the CEO has bad politics
- Tribal political analysis where the other side can never be right about anything
- Dismissing an entire field, methodology, or asset class because of who else uses it

**Antidote:** "If your enemy says X is true, the truth value of X is unaffected." Munger forced himself to read sources he disliked, precisely to neutralize this.

---

## 4. Doubt-Avoidance Tendency

The brain is built to remove doubt by reaching a conclusion fast. This causes people to make decisions before properly considering them, especially under stress or social pressure.

**How it shows up:**
- High-pressure sales tactics that work because they trigger this
- Snap hires after a single interview
- "I just have to decide already" — this often means "I will pick whichever option ends the discomfort"
- Premature investment commitments

**Antidote:** Recognize the discomfort of uncertainty as *information*, not as a problem to be eliminated. Munger's rule: when the urge to decide is strongest, that's often the time to slow down.

---

## 5. Inconsistency-Avoidance Tendency

Once you've taken a position — especially publicly — you defend it against new evidence. The bigger the public commitment, the bigger the distortion. This is also why first impressions are so sticky.

**How it shows up:**
- Investors who keep adding to losers because they can't admit they were wrong
- A CEO doubling down on a failing strategy because they staked their reputation on it
- Defending your candidate / pick / thesis past the point of evidence
- Ideological capture — the more you've said something out loud, the harder it is to say "I was wrong"

**Antidote:** Pre-commit to changing your mind on specific evidence ("I'll sell if free cash flow goes negative for two consecutive quarters"). Make it cheaper to update than to defend.

---

## 6. Curiosity Tendency

The good news: curiosity is one of the few biases on Munger's list that helps. The curious investigate more, get more pieces of the puzzle, and avoid more bullshit.

**How it shows up (in detection):** A genuinely curious person asks questions. Promotional people only talk. Lack of curiosity in a CEO, partner, or advisor is itself a red flag — they have nothing left to learn, which means they have stopped learning.

**Antidote:** Cultivate it deliberately. Be suspicious of your own confidence in topics you haven't recently looked into.

---

## 7. Kantian Fairness Tendency

People expect fair dealing — and reciprocate when they get it, retaliate when they don't. This is why one-sided "great deals" tend to blow up: the other side eventually finds a way out.

**How it shows up:**
- Contracts that are technically enforceable but obviously one-sided — counterparty looks for any way to escape
- Employment relationships where one side is being ripped off — they leave the moment they can
- Investments where management is enriching itself at shareholder expense — eventually the talent leaves or the regulators arrive

**Antidote:** Buffett: "The best deals are the ones both sides are happy with." If you're winning too hard, it isn't durable.

---

## 8. Envy/Jealousy Tendency

Munger: "It is not greed that drives the world, it is envy." People will accept absolute losses to deny others relative gains, and chase what others have even when it doesn't fit their own situation.

**How it shows up:**
- Buying into a bubble because your neighbor / colleague / brother-in-law is making money
- "Keeping up with the Joneses" investing
- FOMO into crypto, NFTs, AI stocks, meme stocks
- Career moves driven by envy of a peer's title rather than fit with your own life

**Antidote:** Munger: "I think the best way to deal with the temptations of envy is to live a life where you are not in close contact with people who have what you want." If you must be in contact, force yourself to evaluate the *full life*, not just the visible win.

---

## 9. Reciprocation Tendency

We feel obligated to return favors, hospitality, gifts, even concessions. This is exploited everywhere — by salespeople, lobbyists, dealmakers.

**How it shows up:**
- Free dinner from a financial advisor to "discuss your portfolio"
- Conference junkets paid for by sell-side
- A CEO buttering up an analyst before earnings
- Equity research funded by the company being researched

**Antidote:** Refuse the favor whenever possible. Munger's advice: be aware that the obligation is automatic, and discount accordingly when accepting hospitality is unavoidable.

---

## 10. Influence-from-Mere-Association Tendency

We judge things by what they're associated with — past successes color future expectations, celebrity endorsements color products, prestigious affiliations color people.

**How it shows up:**
- "Ex-Google" or "ex-Goldman" treated as a marker of competence regardless of actual contribution
- A failed founder's next venture getting funded because the last one had a blip of success
- A stock pumped because a famous investor owns it
- Brand laundering — a sketchy company associating itself with credible names (advisors, board members, "as featured in")

**Antidote:** Evaluate the actual thing on its actual merits. Strip the brand associations and ask whether anything is left.

---

## 11. Simple, Pain-Avoiding Psychological Denial

Reality is sometimes too painful to bear, so the brain distorts it. This is not lying; this is sincere belief in something false because the truth would hurt.

**How it shows up:**
- Parents who genuinely cannot see a child's serious problem
- Investors who cannot accept that a long-held position is broken
- CEOs who genuinely believe in their pivot story even as the numbers say otherwise
- People who stay in clearly bad relationships because the truth is worse than denial

**Antidote:** Notice when *you* have a strong emotional reason not to believe something. That's often where the truth is hiding.

---

## 12. Excessive Self-Regard Tendency

People overrate themselves, their kin, their possessions, and their decisions. This causes endowment effects (overvaluing what you own) and overconfidence.

**How it shows up:**
- Founders convinced their company is undervalued because *they* run it
- People who can't sell a losing stock because *they* picked it
- "My kid is gifted" — said by 90% of parents
- Hiring decisions where the manager picks people who remind them of themselves

**Antidote:** Force decisions through a process that doesn't depend on your judgment alone — a checklist, a partner, a written falsifiability condition.

---

## 13. Overoptimism Tendency

Demosthenes: "Nothing is easier than self-deceit, for what each man wishes, that he also believes to be true." We systematically overestimate good outcomes, especially for our own plans.

**How it shows up:**
- Startup financial projections (always hockey-stick, always wrong)
- Renovation cost estimates
- Project timelines
- Self-assessment of how diligent you'll be with the new diet / habit / strategy
- Wedding budgets

**Antidote:** Look at base rates. What % of startups in this category actually achieve those projections? What % of renovations come in on budget? Discount accordingly. Pre-mortems work — write the failure post-mortem *before* you start, then look for warning signs.

---

## 14. Deprival-Superreaction Tendency

People react more strongly to the threat of loss than to the prospect of equivalent gain. This is loss aversion plus the endowment effect plus the "near miss" effect.

**How it shows up:**
- Holding losers too long ("I'll sell when I get back to break-even")
- Selling winners too soon (locking in the gain you'd hate to lose)
- Refusing to walk away from sunk costs
- Limited-time offers exploiting fear of missing out
- Negotiations where one side gets enraged not by what they're losing but by losing it *almost*

**Antidote:** The only thing that matters is the future expected value from here. The past — what you paid, what you nearly had — is a sunk cost. Munger forces himself to think this way explicitly.

---

## 15. Social-Proof Tendency

We look to others to figure out what's right, especially under uncertainty or stress. The bigger the crowd, the stronger the pull.

**How it shows up:**
- Bubbles. All bubbles.
- "Everyone's buying this stock"
- "Everyone's getting an MBA / a Tesla / a house / a Cybertruck"
- Hiring panels converging on the candidate the loudest member liked
- Internet pile-ons where individual judgment shuts off entirely

**Antidote:** Buffett: "Be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful." The crowd's confidence should reduce yours, not increase it. When you find yourself agreeing because everyone else does, force yourself to articulate the case from first principles.

---

## 16. Contrast-Misreaction Tendency

We judge things by comparison to recent reference points, not absolute value. This is why salespeople show you the expensive option first, and why a small bad thing right after a big good thing barely registers.

**How it shows up:**
- Real estate: a $1M house feels cheap after looking at $1.5M ones
- "Anchor pricing" in retail
- A frog in slowly heated water — small bad changes accumulate unnoticed
- A relationship that has gradually gotten worse seems "normal" because today is only a little worse than yesterday

**Antidote:** Force absolute comparisons. Would you buy this house if it were the *first* one you saw? Would you tolerate this behavior in a person if you met them today?

---

## 17. Stress-Influence Tendency

Under stress, judgment degrades. Under acute stress, people make decisions they would not endorse calmly. Sustained stress (depression) impairs decision quality across the board.

**How it shows up:**
- Panic selling at market bottoms
- Snap commitments under emotional pressure
- High-stakes decisions made under sleep deprivation, grief, illness, or drug influence
- Salespeople deliberately creating urgency to push you into the stress zone

**Antidote:** Slow down whenever you notice you are being rushed. The discomfort of waiting is much smaller than the cost of a stressed decision. Buffett's rule: don't make permanent decisions in temporary states.

---

## 18. Availability-Misweighing Tendency

We weight evidence by how easily it comes to mind, not by how representative it is. Vivid, recent, emotional examples loom larger than statistically more important ones.

**How it shows up:**
- Plane crashes get massive coverage; car deaths don't — so people fear flying
- The most recent market move dominates investors' expectations of the next one
- A founder you just heard a great podcast about feels more compelling than ones you didn't
- Recency bias in performance evaluations

**Antidote:** Ask: what evidence am I *not* seeing because it's quieter? Look at base rates and long-run data, not just the headline.

---

## 19. Use-It-or-Lose-It Tendency

Skills atrophy without practice — including the skill of clear thinking. People who stop using their judgment lose it.

**How it shows up:** Senior people who haven't done the actual work in 20 years can sound confident while being completely wrong. The confidence is intact; the skill underneath is gone.

**Antidote:** Keep doing the actual work, including the parts you've delegated. Munger and Buffett both kept reading and analyzing primary documents personally well into their 90s for exactly this reason.

---

## 20. Drug-Misinfluence Tendency

Substances — alcohol, prescription drugs, recreational drugs — degrade judgment, often without the user noticing. Includes prescription medications with cognitive side effects.

**How it shows up:**
- Decisions made over drinks
- Founders or executives whose behavior is being warped by undisclosed substance issues
- Investors using stimulants or sedatives to push through decisions they should sleep on

**Antidote:** Don't make important decisions impaired. Be alert to signs that someone you're evaluating is operating impaired (this is a very real founder/CEO failure mode).

---

## 21. Senescence-Misinfluence Tendency

Cognitive decline with age, often invisible to the person experiencing it. This isn't only about the elderly — it's about anyone whose judgment is degrading and who doesn't know.

**How it shows up:** Once-great decision-makers continuing to make consequential decisions past the point where they should. Companies, families, and institutions damaged by deference to a leader who is no longer at full capacity.

**Antidote:** Build succession and check-and-balance structures *before* they're needed. Don't trust self-assessment of cognitive decline; use external metrics.

---

## 22. Authority-Misinfluence Tendency

People defer to authority figures past the point where the deference is warranted. Milgram experiments. Aircraft accidents where the first officer wouldn't contradict the captain.

**How it shows up:**
- "The CEO said so" — even when the CEO is obviously wrong
- "The doctor / lawyer / accountant said so" — without checking the actual reasoning
- Cult-of-personality leaders whose subordinates lose the ability to push back
- Boards that rubber-stamp founder decisions

**Antidote:** Evaluate the *argument*, not the person making it. A genius is sometimes wrong; a junior person is sometimes right. The hierarchy of who is talking is irrelevant to the truth value of what they're saying.

---

## 23. Twaddle Tendency

Munger's word for it: people produce a lot of meaningless verbal output, especially in groups, and this twaddle crowds out real thinking.

**How it shows up:**
- Corporate jargon ("synergies," "leverage our core competencies," "value-add")
- Long meetings where nothing is decided
- Pundits who fill airtime regardless of having anything to say
- "Visionary" leaders whose vision turns out to be all words and no specifics
- Long, beautifully formatted decks that don't survive the question "what does this actually mean?"

**Antidote:** Force concrete language. "What specifically? By when? With what resources? At what cost?" If the answers are vague, the thinking is vague.

---

## 24. Reason-Respecting Tendency

People comply more readily when they're given a reason — even a *bad* reason. Famous experiment: people let strangers cut in line at copiers more often if the strangers gave any reason at all, even a meaningless one ("because I have to make copies").

**How it shows up:**
- Salespeople and politicians attaching reasons to requests, knowing the reason itself doesn't have to make sense
- Compliance professionals weaponizing this systematically
- Bullshit reasoning that's accepted because the form of "because X" satisfies the brain's reason-craving

**Antidote:** Evaluate the *content* of the reason, not just its presence. "Because" doesn't make a claim true.

---

## 25. Lollapalooza Tendency — Multiple Biases Acting in Confluence

The most dangerous category. When several biases reinforce each other, the result is not additive but multiplicative. This is how cults, bubbles, frauds, and mass delusions work.

**Anatomy of a lollapalooza:**
- Authority (the leader)
- Social proof (everyone else is in)
- Liking (charismatic spokesperson)
- Reciprocation (free dinners, gifts, preferential access)
- Inconsistency-avoidance (you've already committed publicly)
- Deprival-superreaction (don't miss out, this is going away)
- Reason-respecting (a story that sounds plausible if you don't probe)

**Examples:** Theranos. WeWork. FTX. Madoff. Most multi-level marketing. Most religious cults. Several recent IPOs.

**Antidote:** When you find yourself in a situation where 4+ of these biases are operating simultaneously, *stop*. Step out. Cool off. The lollapalooza is the most reliable signal that you are about to do something stupid that will look obvious in hindsight.

---

## How to Use This File

When you've spotted bullshit, name the bias. Specificity is power. "This is a textbook lollapalooza — Authority + Social Proof + Reciprocation + Deprival-Superreaction" is more useful than "this seems sketchy." It tells the user *exactly* what their brain is doing and why they're being moved.

Munger memorized these because the names themselves became defenses: once you can name the manipulation as it's happening, the manipulation loses most of its power.
